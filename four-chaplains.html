The Four Chaplains

When a U-boat torpedoed the troopship Dorchester, four Army chaplains, a Methodist, a Catholic, a Jew and a Reformed minister, handed their own life jackets to young soldiers. Survivors saw them on the sinking deck, arms linked, praying together.

Act III · World War II

1945

Two stories of faith under fire. In the early hours of February 3, 1943, the transport ship Dorchester was torpedoed off Greenland with about 900 men aboard; 672 died in the freezing water. Four chaplains, George Fox, a Methodist; Alexander Goode, a rabbi; John Washington, a Catholic priest; and Clark Poling, of the Reformed Church in America, calmed the panicking men, handed out life jackets, and when the supply ran out, gave away their own. Survivors remembered seeing them on the slanting deck, arms linked, praying and singing hymns as the ship went down. Two years later on Okinawa, Private First Class Desmond Doss, a Seventh-day Adventist who would not carry a rifle, stayed atop the Maeda Escarpment, which soldiers called Hacksaw Ridge, after his unit was driven back, and lowered roughly 75 wounded men down the cliff by rope. He became the first conscientious objector awarded the Medal of Honor in World War II. The verse on this slide is from the Gospel of John, in the English Standard Version.
