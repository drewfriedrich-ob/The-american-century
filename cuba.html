October 1962 · Cuban Missile Crisis

Thirteen days

The closest the world has come to nuclear war.

Day 1 · Oct 16

President Kennedy is shown spy-plane photos of Soviet nuclear missile sites in Cuba.

Day 7 · Oct 22

He tells the nation and orders a naval “quarantine” around Cuba.

Day 9 · Oct 24

U.S. forces go to DEFCON 2. Soviet ships approach the line, then stop.

Day 12 · Oct 27, “Black Saturday”

A U-2 is shot down over Cuba. On a Soviet submarine, officer Vasili Arkhipov refuses to approve firing a nuclear torpedo.

Day 13 · Oct 28

Khrushchev agrees to remove the missiles. Quietly, the U.S. agrees to pull its own from Turkey.

Act IV · The Cold War

1962

In October 1962, an American U-2 spy plane photographed Soviet medium-range nuclear missile sites under construction in Cuba, just 90 miles from Florida. The red rings show roughly how far those missiles could reach: the inner ring covers Washington; the outer ring, for the longer-range missiles the Soviets also planned, covers most of the country. Kennedy rejected advice to bomb the sites immediately and chose a naval blockade, which he called a quarantine. On October 24, with Soviet ships approaching the line, Secretary of State Dean Rusk whispered, “We’re eyeball to eyeball, and I think the other fellow just blinked.” Then came Black Saturday, October 27: an American U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing Major Rudolf Anderson. That same day, U.S. ships dropped signaling depth charges on a Soviet submarine whose officers, out of contact with Moscow, considered firing a nuclear torpedo. One officer, Vasili Arkhipov, refused to agree. Many historians believe he may have prevented a nuclear war. The next morning, Khrushchev announced the missiles would be removed. In 1963 a hotline was set up between Washington and Moscow.
