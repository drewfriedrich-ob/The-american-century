Act III · World War II

1942

America’s greatest weapon may have been its factories. Car plants switched to tanks and bombers; Ford’s Willow Run plant eventually turned out a B-24 bomber about every hour. Shipyards built 2,710 Liberty ships; one, the Robert E. Peary, was assembled in just over four days as a publicity effort. Rationing of gasoline, sugar, meat and rubber touched every household, and families planted victory gardens and bought war bonds. Millions of women went to work in factories and shipyards, the “Rosie the Riveters,” and about 350,000 served in uniform. The war also exposed America’s contradictions. Black Americans fought for freedom abroad in a segregated military, which fed the civil rights movement after the war. Navajo Marines used their language as an unbreakable code. And after Pearl Harbor, Executive Order 9066 forced about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of them American citizens, from their homes into camps, even as their sons in the 442nd Regimental Combat Team fought heroically in Europe. In 1988, Congress formally apologized and paid reparations to survivors.
