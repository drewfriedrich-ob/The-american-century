June 25, 1950 – July 27, 1953

Korea: the war that never officially ended

North Korea, backed by Stalin and Mao, invades the South. With the Soviets boycotting the Security Council, the UN votes to resist. 16 nations send combat troops.

The front swings wildly: from the Pusan Perimeter, to MacArthur’s landing at Inchon, to the Chinese border, and back as China pours in hundreds of thousands of troops.

Two more years of bloody stalemate end in an armistice near where it began. No peace treaty was ever signed.

Act IV · The Cold War

1950

Korea had been split at the 38th parallel in 1945, with the Soviets occupying the north and Americans the south. On June 25, 1950, North Korea invaded with Soviet tanks and Stalin’s approval. Watch the map: within weeks, American and South Korean troops were pinned in a small corner around the port of Pusan. Then, on September 15, General Douglas MacArthur landed behind enemy lines at Inchon, near Seoul, one of the boldest operations in military history. UN forces raced north toward the Chinese border at the Yalu River. In late 1950, China intervened with massive force; Marines fought their way out of the frozen Chosin Reservoir in temperatures far below zero, and Seoul fell again in January 1951. When MacArthur publicly pushed to widen the war into China, President Truman fired him, asserting civilian control of the military. The war ground on for two more years until an armistice on July 27, 1953, left the border near the 38th parallel. Today’s Korean War Veterans Memorial in Washington carries a simple inscription: Freedom Is Not Free. South Korea went on to become one of the world’s most prosperous democracies.
