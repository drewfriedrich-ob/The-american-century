Prologue · 1898

The year America stepped onto the world stage

In one summer, an inward-looking nation became a power with territory across two oceans.

“A splendid little war”

After the USS Maine exploded in Havana harbor, the U.S. fought Spain and won in about four months, taking Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines.

July 1898

A Pacific foothold

Congress annexed Hawaii. Pearl Harbor would become the Navy’s great Pacific base, and, 43 years later, a target.

The big stick

Act I · The deep causes

Europe in 1914 was a powder keg

Historians sum up the long-burning causes in four letters.

The great powers raced to build armies and navies, and every general had a war plan ready.

A

Two armed camps. A fight with one could mean a fight with all.

I

Act I · Motives

Why each nation went to war

Allied Powers

Serbia

Defend its independence against an Austro-Hungarian invasion.

Russia

Protect fellow Slavs in Serbia and its standing as a great power.

France

Stand by its ally Russia, and win back Alsace-Lorraine, lost to Germany in 1871.

Britain

Defend neutral Belgium, which it had promised to protect, and stop any one power from dominating Europe.

Central Powers

Austria-Hungary

Punish Serbia and crush the Slavic nationalism threatening to break its empire apart.

Germany

Back its one dependable ally, and strike before Russia and France grew stronger.

Ottoman Empire

Join Germany against its old enemy Russia, and recover lost prestige.

Bulgaria

Win back land it had lost to Serbia in the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913.

Light in the darkness

Christmas 1914: “Silent Night” in no man’s land

On Christmas Eve, along stretches of the Western Front, German soldiers set candlelit trees on their trench walls and sang Stille Nacht. British soldiers answered with carols of their own.

By morning, men who had been trying to kill each other climbed out to shake hands, trade gifts, bury their dead and, in places, kick a football.

For a few hours, the Prince of Peace was honored on a battlefield. It never happened on that scale again.

June 28, 1919 · Exactly five years after Sarajevo

A peace that planted the next war

The Treaty of Versailles

War guilt. Germany must accept blame for the war.

Reparations. Payments later set at 132 billion gold marks.

Land. Germany loses about 13% of its European territory and all its colonies.

Arms. Its army is capped at 100,000 men, with no air force and no tanks.

Wilson’s dream, a League of Nations, was born. But the U.S. Senate rejected the treaty, and America never joined.

Meanwhile, in a German army hospital

A 29-year-old corporal, temporarily blinded by a British gas attack, learned that Germany had surrendered. He blamed traitors at home, and he never forgave the peace.

His name was Adolf Hitler.

June 6, 1944 · Bedford, Virginia

One small town, one terrible morning

Bedford, a Virginia town of about 3,200 people, lost 19 of its sons on D-Day. Most served together in Company A, 116th Infantry, in the first wave at Omaha Beach.

No American community is believed to have lost more, for its size, that day. It is why the National D-Day Memorial stands in Bedford.

1933–1945 · Never forget

The Holocaust

6,000,000

Jewish men, women and children were murdered by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, about two of every three Jews in Europe.

Millions of others were also killed: Roma, people with disabilities, Polish and Soviet civilians, prisoners of war, and others the regime deemed unworthy of life.

April 1945 · Liberation

American soldiers who opened camps like Buchenwald and Dachau could barely describe what they found. General Eisenhower ordered it all filmed and documented so no one could ever deny it.

The Righteous Among the Nations

More than 28,000 non-Jews have been honored for risking their lives to save Jews, among them Corrie ten Boom, whose Christian family hid refugees in their home in the Netherlands.

Light in the darkness

Courage that would not let go of faith

February 3, 1943 · North Atlantic

The Four Chaplains

When a U-boat torpedoed the troopship Dorchester, four Army chaplains, a Methodist, a Catholic, a Jew and a Reformed minister, handed their own life jackets to young soldiers. Survivors saw them on the sinking deck, arms linked, praying together.

May 1945 · Okinawa

Desmond Doss

A medic who refused, out of Christian conviction, to carry a weapon. Under fire on a jagged cliff, he lowered about 75 wounded men to safety, one at a time, praying, “Lord, please help me get one more.”

June 25, 1950 – July 27, 1953

Korea: the war that never officially ended

North Korea, backed by Stalin and Mao, invades the South. With the Soviets boycotting the Security Council, the UN votes to resist. 16 nations send combat troops.

The front swings wildly: from the Pusan Perimeter, to MacArthur’s landing at Inchon, to the Chinese border, and back as China pours in hundreds of thousands of troops.

Two more years of bloody stalemate end in an armistice near where it began. No peace treaty was ever signed.
