Christmas 1914: “Silent Night” in no man’s land

On Christmas Eve, along stretches of the Western Front, German soldiers set candlelit trees on their trench walls and sang Stille Nacht. British soldiers answered with carols of their own.

By morning, men who had been trying to kill each other climbed out to shake hands, trade gifts, bury their dead and, in places, kick a football.

For a few hours, the Prince of Peace was honored on a battlefield. It never happened on that scale again.

This is the first of our Light in the Darkness slides. By December 1914 the war of movement was over and the armies had dug in. Then, on Christmas Eve, something remarkable happened along parts of the British and German lines in Belgium and France. German troops placed small Christmas trees with candles on their parapets and sang carols. British troops sang back. On Christmas Day, soldiers met in no man’s land, exchanged cigarettes, food and buttons, buried their dead together, and in a few places played informal games of football. The truce was not universal, and it was not repeated on that scale: commanders on both sides were alarmed and forbade fraternization in later years. But for a moment, the shared faith of men on both sides proved stronger than the orders that divided them. It is a reminder that even in the worst places, hope can break through.
